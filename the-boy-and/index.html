The Lab

"Nani!!!"

"Le, Nani. The water's hot. Come, take bath."

His mother's voice came from far away, then closer, then right beside his ear.

Nani. That's what everyone at home called him. He was seven.

He pulled the bedsheet over his head. Then he remembered.

It was Diwali.

He threw the sheet off and sat up. The house already smelled like a festival. Dosas crisping on the tawa. And under that, something sweeter. Milk and ghee and cardamom, the slow, warm smell of payasam bubbling on the stove.

A festival, and a holiday on top of it. There was no better kind of day.

He was old enough to bathe himself on most days. But not on festival days. On festival days, it was a proper head bath, the traditional kind. Warm oil rubbed into his hair, then a paste of amla, reetha and shikakai that his mother scrubbed in with strong fingers, and that always, always found its way into his eyes.

"Kallu moosko," she said. Close your eyes. He squeezed them shut and held his breath while she poured mug after mug of hot water over his head.

Halfway through, she ran. "Ayyo, the dosa!" He stood there dripping, eyes shut tight, listening to the dosa sizzle and the clatter of the ladle in the kitchen, until she came hurrying back to finish the job.

She rubbed his head dry so hard he thought it might come off, and ran back to the kitchen. A minute later she was back again, this time with new clothes in her hands. A cream kurta with a thin gold border and a pyjama to match, still crisp from the shop, with a small dab of turmeric at the edges. Like all new clothes, they had been kept at the pooja first, to be blessed.

He put them on and stood a little taller.

She held his face by the cheeks to keep his head still, and combed his hair with quick, firm strokes. A neat side parting. One last look.

"Ayyindi. Go."

And she was gone again, back to her stove.

He went straight to the common room. His father and his elder sister were already dressed and sitting in front of the TV. His sister looked up, looked at his neatly combed hair, and smirked. He ignored her. He had more important things to do.

Because in the corner of the common room was a cupboard.

To everyone else in the house, it was just a cupboard. To him, it was the lab.

Inside, lined up on the shelf, were his pencil boxes. A red one, a blue one, one shaped like a car, a few transparent ones. Each was home to a specimen.

He opened the first one. In the corner, on a thin web, sat a spider so green it looked painted. Around it, here and there, the empty shells of house flies hung in the silk. He watched it for a while, then closed the lid.

In the next box, a few spotted ladybirds, some big, some small, crawled over each other like tiny buttons.

Then he went out to the garden.

The sun was barely up. The backyard was full of the plants his family grew: flowers, vegetables, a few small trees. The air still had the cool of the night in it, and the grass was wet under his feet. He had been told, very clearly, not to get his new clothes dirty. He was being very careful. Mostly.

He walked slowly, bending under leaves, peering into corners.

After a while, he found it. A web stretched between two stems, heavy with dew, every thread shining. At the centre sat a spider. Slim, long, black and glossy.

He ran back inside, picked out an empty pencil box, and came back.

He shook the web gently. The spider woke and started to move away. He shook it again. And again. After a few more interruptions, the spider gave up on its web altogether and crawled onto a rose plant nearby.

Now it was slow. Now it was his.

He held the open box steady against a leaf and, with one finger, tapped softly near the spider. Once. Twice. Guiding it, little by little, until it stepped inside.

Snap. The lid closed.

He carried it back to the lab like a trophy.

There was one more thing to check. On another plant, hanging from the underside of a leaf, was a green pupa. He had been watching it for days. In his notebook, he had drawn a chart for it in pencil. The lines were wobbly, the boxes were all different sizes, and the numbers got smaller and more squashed towards the end, where he had run out of space. Day 1 to Day 21.

He crossed out Day 12.

Nine more days. Then a butterfly.

"Nani! Tiffin cheddam ra!" his mother called.

The dosas were crisp, the chutney was fresh, and the payasam was everything he had been dreaming of since he woke up. After that came cartoons, until his sister won the remote. Then Nat Geo. Then the long, slow wait for evening.

By evening, the whole house was glowing. Rows of small clay diyas flickered along the terrace wall and down the front steps. Then the street burst into light and noise. Crackers bursting, sparklers spinning, flowerpots fountaining gold into the dark.

And after every cracker came that smell. Sharp and smoky, hanging in the air, clinging to his new clothes, still there in his hair when he finally went to bed.

A few days later, it was Sunday.

Somehow, on Sundays, he always woke up before anyone else in the house. He would lie there for a moment, confused, and then remember. It's Sunday. And the thought would fill him with a quiet joy.

He brushed his teeth and went up to the terrace. Then he just sat there. Doing nothing.

From a temple far away, the Suprabhatam floated over the rooftops. The sky was clear and pale, and birds crossed it in small groups, calling to each other.

His favourite part of Sunday was the creeper. It climbed all the way from the garden up to the terrace wall, covered in flowers, and every morning tiny hummingbirds came to it. They hovered in front of each flower, wings a blur, heads perfectly still, moving from one blossom to the next, one by one. He could watch them for hours.

After a while, he went down, watched some TV, and then, of course, it was lab time. He checked on every specimen, one box at a time.

In the evening, around five, he took the leftover Diwali crackers and went out to the garden.

He knew exactly where to go. In one corner, the ground was dotted with the small holes of black ants.

He had saved a special cracker for this. Not the kind that burst. A thin one, long and flexible like a piece of thread, that didn't make a sound at all. It just burned slowly, fizzing with tiny sparks all the way down.

He pushed it deep into one of the holes, leaving only the end of the thread sticking out. Then he lit it and stepped back.

The sparks crawled down the thread and disappeared into the earth.

For a moment, nothing.

Then smoke began to rise out of the hole. And after the smoke, the ants. Pouring out, dozens of them, running in every direction.

Some of them weren't running. Some were burnt grey, dragging themselves along, their legs not working the way they should.

Those were the ones he picked up. They were easier to catch, and they didn't bite.

He put a few of them in an empty matchbox, dug a small pit, and buried it. Then he opened his notebook, wrote down the spot and the date, drew a new wobbly chart from Day 1 to Day 14, and crossed out Day 1.

On his way back in, he crossed out another day on the butterfly chart.

Every evening after school, he helped his grandmother in the garden.

They watered the plants, pulled out weeds, cut back the branches that had grown too far. She showed him how to make a paadu, a small ring of mud around each plant so the water would stay where it was needed. She knew the name of every plant and the habits of every insect, and he followed her around asking questions until she laughed and told him to go and play.

When he did play, it was usually in the guava tree. It was the best place in the whole garden to find interesting insects.

Sometimes he took an old injection syringe from his lab and used the needle to cut the stems of certain plants, the ones that bled a thick white milk. He would draw the milk into the syringe and inject it into other plants, just to see what would happen. Sometimes he fed it to the spiders and insects in his lab.

Everything in that garden amazed him. But his favourite thing wasn't a flower or a fruit. It was a seed.

He loved the days right after planting. Nothing but empty soil. One day. Two days. Three. Nothing. And then one morning, out of nowhere, a tiny green bud would be standing there, as if it had always been there.

That was the moment that fascinated him the most. Something that had been growing the whole time, quietly, where no one could see.

The garden had its dangers too. He had been stung by honeybees more times than he could count. But the one sting he never forgot was a hornet's. After that, whenever he saw one, he ran out of the garden and hid in his room.

The next weekend, he dug up the matchbox.

Inside were the ants, dead now. And on their bodies, tiny transparent creatures, so small he had to bring his face close to see them move.

He wrote it all down in his notebook. Then he buried the matchbox again and added more days to the chart.

A week after that, Day 21 had come and gone, and there was still no butterfly. He ran out of patience with the pupa.

He wanted to see what was happening inside. So he took a paper blade and, very carefully, cut it open.

There was no butterfly. Only liquid, and something soft and shapeless that was not yet anything at all.

His grandmother found him there, holding the two halves.

She didn't shout. She just looked at the pupa for a long time, and then at him. Somehow, that was worse.

"Tappu, Nani," she said quietly. That's wrong.

She sat down beside him.

"Some things take their own time. You can't hurry them. You wait for your seeds every day, don't you? This is the same."

He didn't have an answer. He just sat there, looking at what he had done, not quite understanding why it felt so heavy.

That night, he went to sleep. By the next evening, he was back in the garden, back in the guava tree, back in the lab.

Life, back then, was full of innocence and curiosity.

The City

Eighteen years later.

Time had a way of slipping by quietly.

One day he was a boy with mud under his nails, climbing the guava tree in his grandmother's garden. The next, he was a grown man, hustling in the middle of a busy city that hummed with ambition.

He lived on the tenth floor, the top one, in a small flat with an open terrace. He had fallen in love with it the moment he saw it.

The mornings there were soft. The sunlight came in so gently that even waking up felt warm. Some days he took his coffee out to the terrace and just stood there for a while, doing nothing, the way he used to as a boy.

He made his own breakfast. Sometimes oatmeal, sometimes bread and peanut butter, sometimes nothing but strong coffee that filled the whole flat with its roasted smell. He enjoyed these little things.

The office bus picked him up at the corner. He always took a window seat, earbuds in, head leaning against the glass, the music mixing with the city outside. Honking autos, flower sellers, people rushing, sunlight bouncing off glass buildings. Every day, the bus crawled past the same half-built flyover. It had been under construction for so long that nobody asked when it would be finished anymore. By now, it was just meme material.

On rainy days, the rain drummed on the roof of the bus and ran down the glass in long, wobbly lines. Sometimes, stuck in traffic with the rain coming down, he thought of the garden back home after rain, and the smell of wet earth. Then the traffic moved, and the thought moved with it.

He loved his job. He loved solving problems, building things, watching ideas click together like gears finally locking into place. His colleagues joked that he glowed a little when he explained something he was excited about. Maybe he did.

There were hard days too. Deadlines. Expectations. That unspoken race everyone seemed to be running. But even then, he found small pockets of joy. The warm, sweet smell of waffles drifting out of the little shop near his stop. Filter coffee in a steel tumbler, poured from a height until it frothed. The pink trees of AECS Layout in late winter, a treat for the eyes. And once in a while, a small miracle: an evening with almost no traffic, the bus actually moving, cool air pouring in through the window all the way home.

He never thought of adulthood as sad. Busy, yes. Overwhelming, sometimes. But full. Full of flavours, colours, noise, and stories.

And the weekends belonged to his friends, mostly spent at his place.

Everyone talked at once. Someone chopped onions and cried about it. Someone else insisted there was no such thing as too much masala. Rice steaming, curries bubbling, someone counting the whistles of the pressure cooker over all the laughter. They cooked like a small, chaotic family.

And like every family, they had their rules.

When their friend from college made his famous biryani, they had learned to read his face. If he tasted it and said, "This time it's really good," everyone quietly opened Swiggy. If he frowned and said, "Hmm. Not great this time," they all rushed for plates.

And there was always that one friend who, no matter when anyone called him, was "fifteen more minutes" away. He never chopped a single onion, but somehow he walked in exactly when the food was ready. "Arre, why didn't you call me earlier?" he would say, already sitting down to eat. "I would have helped."

Nobody believed him. Everybody saved him a plate anyway.

On Saturday evenings, they went out for football. Floodlights, a rented turf, two hours of running until their lungs burned and their legs felt like jelly. Then, sweaty and starving, back to his place.

Late at night, they sat on the terrace with a deck of cards or a game of Monopoly Deal, the city glittering below them, accusing each other of cheating, nobody wanting to be the first to go home.

Some Sunday mornings, they went out hunting for breakfast. A long queue at Rameshwaram Cafe for ghee podi idli. Or a squeezed-in table at MTR for a crisp benne masala dosa, at a place older than most of their parents.

And there were the movie nights. Lights off, cushions all over the floor, a long argument about what to watch, and then, finally, a movie.

The Fable

That Saturday, they put on S/O Satyamurthy.

Most of them had seen it before. A South Indian family film with a good mix of comedy and emotion, the kind that makes you laugh for ten minutes and then quietly gets you. Half the group talked through it. The other half pretended to be annoyed.

Early in the film came the scene.

On screen, Satyamurthy handed money, once again, to a man who had lied to him. His son watched, and finally asked.

"How many times will you get cheated, Nanna?"

Satyamurthy didn't argue. He just told his son a story.

A boy lived with his father at the edge of a forest. One day, just for fun, he screamed, "Tiger! Tiger!" His father came running. There was no tiger. The boy laughed.

A few days later, he did it again. Again his father came running. Again, no tiger.

Then one day, the tiger really came. The boy screamed louder than ever. But his father, tired of being fooled, didn't go.

And the boy was gone.

Everyone in the room knew that story. They had all heard it as children, in one version or another. A wolf, a tiger, a shepherd boy, a village. The lesson was always the same: nobody believes a liar, even when he's telling the truth.

But Satyamurthy saw it differently.

"That father should have gone the third time too, no?" he said. "What would he lose? A few steps. A little effort. Maybe he gets fooled again. That's all."

Satyamurthy looked at his son.

"But if he doesn't go… he loses his son."

Something in that voice, soft and almost breaking, carried off the screen and into the flat. For a second, the room went quiet. Even the ones who had been talking.

Then someone reached for the chips, and everyone went back to watching casually.

On the floor, his back against the sofa, he watched differently.

Not dramatically. No tears. Just a faint tightening in his chest, a sense of something shifting that hadn't yet taken shape. He didn't know why it stayed with him. It settled somewhere inside, like a seed dropped into soil.

He didn't say anything. He leaned back, kept watching, laughed at the next scene with everyone else, and went to bed that night like any other night.

But the line stayed.

Quiet.

Waiting.

Five Seconds

The next morning began like any other Sunday. Bright sunlight. Leftover curry in the fridge. The pleasant laziness of a day with nowhere to be.

He walked into the bathroom, half asleep, rubbing his eyes.

And there, in the toilet bowl, a tiny ant was swimming in panicked circles.

He stopped.

It was such a small thing. Really, so small.

He had seen hundreds like it before. Most times, he had flushed them away without even noticing.

But today…

The line from the film came back to him, soft as an echo.

What would he lose? A few steps. A little effort…

Before he could think about why, he tore a strip off the cardboard box of a new soap from the shelf and dipped it into the water. The ant grabbed on by instinct, legs trembling, antennae flicking like tiny question marks. He lifted it out and set it down on the dry tiles.

It stood still for a second. Then it walked away.

He didn't think much of it.

Maybe a small smile.

Maybe nothing.

He washed his hands, made breakfast, and went on with his day.

But the next morning, there was another ant.

Same place. Same struggle, legs slicing at the water in frantic little arcs.

He rescued it.

The morning after that, he caught himself looking for one before he had even switched on the light.

Strange, he thought. Why am I expecting this?

He found one. Maybe the same ant. Maybe not.

He rescued it anyway.

After that, it became a small habit. Not every day. Some weeks, there were none at all. And then one morning there would be one again, paddling in circles, and he would reach for the soap box without even thinking.

The soap box never went into the dustbin after that. It stayed on the shelf beside the basin, getting a little shorter every week.

Once, it happened at the worst possible moment.

He had overslept, and gulped down two big cups of coffee while getting ready. Halfway through the second, his bladder stopped making requests and started issuing orders. He rushed to the bathroom.

And there it was. An ant. Doing its slow, desperate circles.

He froze.

"Seriously?" he said to it. "Now?"

The ant did not apologise.

He started a small, urgent dance, hopping from foot to foot, knees pressed together. Every part of him was negotiating. He held on, tore the strip, fished the ant out, and set it down on the tiles in what might have been a world record.

And then, finally, relief. Long, glorious, eyes-closed relief. The kind that makes a grown man sigh out loud in an empty bathroom.

The ant had been saved. So, in a way, had he.

It was a small act. It didn't make him a saint. He didn't think he was saving the world.

But every time he lifted that strip of cardboard, he felt something. A warmth. A pause.

Sometimes, watching an ant find its feet on the tiles, something stirred at the back of his mind. A garden. A matchbox. He never followed the thought very far.

To him, those five seconds were nothing.

To the ant, they were its entire life.

And in that small exchange, a little effort for a whole life, he found a strange, unexpected peace.

He never told his friends about it. It didn't need to be shared.

Some things are too small, and too quiet, to put into words.

A Few Steps

It happened on a Saturday evening.

The floodlights were on. The turf still held the heat of the day. A friendly football match, nothing at stake. But nobody had told the players that.

The score was nil-nil. Twenty minutes left. Every tackle was a little harder than the last.

Out there, the rest of the world switched off. No office. No phone. No tomorrow. Just the slap of the ball, the squeak of boots on turf, someone shouting "Man on!", his own breathing loud in his ears. Sweat stinging his eyes. Legs burning. Everything narrowed down to the ball and the next pass.

He was in midfield, waiting for his chance.

Then he saw it. A gap, opening up right in the middle of the pitch.

He drifted into it.

The left-back looked up and found him.

Before the ball even came, he checked over his shoulder. On the far side, the right-winger was already making his run, sprinting down the line, one arm raised. He knew exactly where the next pass had to go.

The ball came to him low and clean, and reached his feet with his back to the goal. All he had to do was turn.

He took the touch and started to spin.

From his blind side, a boot came sliding in. Studs first. Late and reckless. Straight into his foot.

He heard the crack before he felt it.

Then the pain. Then the ground.

He shut his eyes.

When he opened them, he was lying on the turf. Faces all around him. His teammates. Someone shouting for water. Someone else shouting for a car.

He blinked.

When he opened his eyes again, there were bright white lights above him. Masks. Gloved hands. A voice asking him to count backwards from ten.

He blinked.

When he opened his eyes again, he was lying in his own bed, in his shorts, staring at the ceiling fan turning slowly above him.

A fractured bone in his foot. A small screw to hold it together, a line of stitches, and a cast that ran halfway up his leg. Six weeks on crutches. The foot was not to touch the ground. Not once.

The first time he tried to make tea, he could barely lift the kettle and hold himself up at the same time.

He learned to accept help. His friends came by with boxes of food. The same people who used to take over his flat every weekend now came quietly, filled his fridge, sat with him for a while, and left.

Everything was harder now. The fracture throbbed every time his foot hung down. His hip and lower back ached from hopping on one leg, his whole body leaning the wrong way. Even the simplest things, getting a glass of water, changing his shirt, came with a cost. Every movement was something he had to spend.

The bathroom became a small Everest.

Eleven steps from his bed. He had counted them. On a bad day, it took five minutes.

A few days passed. Then, one early morning, he began the trek again. Crutch. Hop. Breathe. Crutch. Hop. Breathe.

He reached the bathroom, leaned against the wall to catch his breath, and lifted the lid.

An ant.

The same circles. The same desperate paddling in the shallow water.

He stood there.

His foot was throbbing. His arms ached from the crutches. The soap box was on the shelf beside the basin, where it had always been. Just a few feet away.

A few feet that, this morning, felt very far.

To get it, he would have to let go of his crutches, balance on one leg with a hand on the basin, and reach across with the other. And then he would have to get down to the bowl. Down onto his good knee, on the wet bathroom floor, keeping the cast up and away from the water.

One slip on those tiles, and six weeks could become six months.

And then, somehow, he would have to get back up.

It would be so easy not to see it. To flush. Or to go back to bed and let the morning take care of it.

He stood there for a long time.

And then, of course, the line came back.

What would he lose? A few steps. A little effort. That's all.

It had been easy to agree with, sprawled on cushions with his friends, a plate of food in his lap.

But this morning, a few steps were the hardest thing he had. And it was not going to be a little effort.

Then the rest of the line came too.

But if he doesn't go…

For him, it would cost some pain and a few minutes of a morning.

For the ant, it was everything.

He leaned his crutches against the wall.

He turned slowly on his good leg, one hand flat on the basin, and reached for the soap box. His fingertips brushed it and pushed it a little further away. He took a breath, leaned a little more, and caught it.

He couldn't tear it with one hand. So he held it in his teeth and pulled.

Then came the hard part.

Gripping the edge of the basin, he lowered himself down, inch by inch, until his good knee touched the floor. The tiles were wet and cold. He kept his injured foot lifted behind him, the cast held clear of the floor, his arm shaking on the basin as it took his weight.

He stayed like that for a moment, breathing hard.

Then he leaned over the bowl and dipped the strip into the water.

The ant missed it. Slipped. Went under for a second and came back up.

He tried again. Slower this time. He held the cardboard still, just at the surface, and waited.

The ant found the edge. Grabbed on. Climbed.

He lifted it out carefully, as if it weighed something, and set the strip down on the one dry tile he could find.

The ant didn't move.

Then, slowly, it cleaned its antennae with its front legs. Took a step. Then another.

And kneeling there, so close to the floor, eye to eye with an ant, he remembered.

The garden. The smoke rising out of the hole. The ants pouring out, running in every direction. And the ones that couldn't run. The burnt ones, dragging themselves along.

He had always picked the struggling ones.

Back then, because they were easy to catch.

Now, because they couldn't get out on their own.

The ant reached the edge of the tile, followed the line between two tiles toward the wall, and disappeared into a crack.

He stayed there a while, longer than he needed to, turning the strip of cardboard over in his fingers. Not because the ant needed it anymore. Because he did.

The world didn't celebrate. The world just continued.

Getting back up took him ten minutes. He didn't mind.

Later, lying in bed and watching the fan turn, he thought about Satyamurthy. He understood him a little better now.

All his life, he had heard it as a story about a boy who lied. Satyamurthy had turned it into a story about the father, and the few steps he didn't take.

Most days, doing the right thing would cost him almost nothing. Five seconds. A strip of cardboard.

Some days, it would cost him a lot more.

And he would never really know how much it meant to the one on the other side.

But he could always take the few steps.

Epilogue

The Window

A winter morning. A couple of months later.

The gym was nearly empty. Most people, it seemed, had chosen their blankets over the treadmill. The music was low. Sunlight came in through the tall windows, bounced off the mirrors, and spread across the floor like something warm being shared.

He was getting his strength back, slowly. One set at a time. The foot still complained sometimes. He had learned to listen to it. A few more weeks, and it would stop complaining altogether.

Between sets, he liked to stand by the window with his face in the sun. Outside, the traffic moved without a sound behind the closed glass, like a film on mute. On a cold morning, that little patch of warmth was the best thing in the room.

That was where he noticed it.

A butterfly.

He had no idea how it had got in. But the windows were shut, and it was throwing itself against the glass again and again, trying to reach a sky it could see but couldn't get to.

He put down his water bottle and stepped closer, still with the faintest limp.

He cupped his hands around it, gently, careful not to press on the wings. It panicked. It fluttered out of his hands and hit the glass again.

He waited.

Some things take their own time.

When it came to rest on the glass, he brought his hands around it again, slowly. This time, it settled. He could feel it in his palms, tiny and trembling, its wings ticking softly against his fingers.

He nudged the window latch open with his elbow, pushed the glass out, and opened his hands.

The butterfly hesitated for a second. Then it was gone. Out into the cold, bright morning, up and over the trees.

He watched it go. For a few seconds, everything was peaceful.

Then he turned around to get back to his workout.

Someone was standing right there.

She must have been there a while. Long enough to have seen the whole thing.

A girl. Eyes so dark you could get lost in them, and never want to be found. And in one corner, a small catch of sunlight, like the touch of light a painter leaves in the eyes of an oil portrait.

She glanced at the open window. Then back at him.

"Hi," she said softly, with a gentle smile.

"I'm Naina."
